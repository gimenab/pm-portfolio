Treasure Time: Reinventing Personal Productivity

A gamified app that turns small everyday actions (health, home, work) into protected time for well-being. This case follows it through the whole project cycle, step by step, from the founder's brief and SOW to building the real app.

1.3Stakeholder alignment

Nine of the 31 intake gaps were picked for the kickoff, ranked by scope-creep risk. There are no outside stakeholders: I am founder, product owner and PM, so every question got a written decision, and the SOW became v2. The decisions that changed the project:

  • Platform: a web app, not a native mobile app.
  • Phase 1 covers the whole core loop except enjoying the time, split in two deliveries. 1a: do tasks, earn minutes, see them accumulate. 1b: take time and register the moment, which closes the loop. Both are scheduled after Discovery.
  • Minutes are the currency, and accumulated time never expires.
  • The points model is evaluated during Phase 1, starting with 1a. Phase 1 signal: used on 5 of 7 days for 2 weeks.
  • Anything outside Phase 1 needs an explicit, written decision before it enters.

Stakeholders

Purpose

(Literal from the source, translated.)

Treasure Time is a gamified mobile app focused on human well-being and on balancing daily obligations with meaningful personal time. It helps people recover quality time for activities that give them real well-being. It does this by turning small everyday actions (health, home, work and functional responsibilities) into protected time for activities the user chooses.

Unlike traditional habit or productivity apps, it does not aim to maximize performance or extreme productivity. It aims to help users sustain their functional life while recovering space to exist as human beings. Its core proposition is: "Transform small daily actions into valuable time to live better."

Problem (from the source). Many adults feel absorbed by work, responsibilities, home upkeep, daily obligations, mental fatigue and passive social media use. They often feel "I have no time for myself", even when they genuinely want to make time for meaningful activities such as reading, walking, painting, knitting, cinema, nature or hobbies. Existing apps focus on habit tracking, productivity, streaks, performance metrics and reducing screen time. Few turn everyday task completion into protected, meaningful time.

Product hypothesis (from the source). If a user transforms daily functional actions into visible, accumulable recovered time, they will be more likely to spend real time on activities that give them well-being. As a result, they will do more meaningful activities and feel better.

MVP objective (from the source). Validate whether users actually recover and use meaningful time in their daily lives.

Possible success metrics (from the source, described as "possible").
- North Star: hours of valuable time recovered per user per week.
- Secondary: frequency of use, protected time used, number of meaningful activities done, reduction in passive consumption, perceived well-being, and weekly retention.

Product philosophy and tone (from the source). This is design intent, not a testable requirement.
- The product should not feel like an endless task list, toxic productivity, "earning the right to rest", or guilt for not complying.
- It should feel like human accompaniment, gentle progress, recovery of vital time, and building personal space.
- The expected emotional tone is warm, human, hopeful, calm, contemplative and non-judgmental.
- The app does not seek to demand more productivity, punish non-compliance or impose extreme discipline.

Target Audience

(Literal from the source.) The initial target audience is functional adults, approximately 25–45 years old, who:
- feel exhausted
- have little personal time
- feel disconnected from hobbies or well-being
- want to balance obligations and personal life
- spend time passively (scrolling, digital distraction)
- want to reconnect with meaningful activities

Scope / Major Project Activities

(From the source's "MVP Inicial" and the sections that detail it. Wording is condensed and translated.)

  • Simple categories. Tasks are grouped into three initial categories to avoid cognitive overload:
  • Health – "Looking after myself". Examples: drink water, walk 10 minutes, sleep well, medication, mindful pause, therapy.
  • Home – "Sustaining my space". Examples: make the bed, wash dishes, tidy up, cook something simple, take out the trash, pay bills and taxes on time.
  • Work / Purpose. Examples: focus block, advance an important task, answer pending items, study, concentrated work.
  • Few configurable tasks. Onboarding is minimal, and the user picks only a few actions to start, with a conceptual maximum of 2–3 tasks per category.
  • Recovered-time system. Completing actions generates "recovered time / valuable time" as the reward, instead of abstract coins. Conceptual examples in the source are make the bed +2 min, walk 15 min +10 min, and focus block +15 min. A daily summary example is "Today you recovered 34 minutes for yourself."
  • Personal activities. The user can invest recovered time in meaningful activities such as reading, painting, walking, knitting, photography, music, journaling, watching a movie, a quiet coffee, nature, and custom hobbies. The app can suggest activities and lets users add their own.
  • Time bank. Listed in the MVP, but the brief does not describe it beyond its name and the "visible, accumulable" recovered time in the hypothesis.
  • Protected mode. When the user decides to use their recovered time, they can activate a protected mode, inspired by focus and app-blocker apps. It may include distracting-app blocking, focus mode, notification silencing, a timer and gentle accompaniment. The example message is "This time you recovered for yourself."
  • Simple emotional reflection. At the end of valuable time, the app asks "How did you feel?" with three options: Good, So-so, Didn't connect today. This supports personalized learning, better suggestions and well-being tracking.

Described in the source, but unclear whether it is in the MVP: the suggestion system. The brief says the app "could" propose activities based on usage history, user preferences, reported well-being and activity frequency (example: "You haven't walked in several days. Want to recover 20 minutes outdoors?"). The MVP list includes "personal activities" but not suggestions explicitly.

Core loop

DO a task → COMPLETE it → earn +X minutes → ACCUMULATE time → CHOOSE what to do with it → ENJOY it → REGISTER the moment → back to DO.

Phase 1a covers DO to ACCUMULATE. Phase 1b adds CHOOSE and REGISTER, which closes the loop. ENJOY happens outside the app.

Minutes are the currency. Time accumulates and never expires. The app always shows the accumulated total with the message "Look at all the time you recovered". When taking time, the user chooses all of it or part of it.

Target Audience

(Unchanged from v1.) The initial target audience is functional adults, approximately 25–45 years old, who feel exhausted, have little personal time, feel disconnected from hobbies or well-being, want to balance obligations and personal life, spend time passively (scrolling, digital distraction), and want to reconnect with meaningful activities.

To be validated in Discovery (step 1.4). There is no research behind this audience yet.

Scope / Major Project Activities

Platform. Web app (React + Vite + Tailwind). No native app and no PWA for now. Features that need a native app wait until the core loop is validated.

Phase 1a — in scope

From DO to ACCUMULATE: the user does tasks and sees the minutes pile up.

  • Three categories: Health, Home, Work / Purpose.
  • A catalog of 10 predefined tasks across the three categories (the split per category is defined in the build). Each day the app pre-loads up to 3 of them. How the 3 are chosen is defined in the build. The predefined tasks cannot be edited or deleted.
  • One custom task per category per day, written by the user and flagged as the difficult one. The exact flow (when the user writes it) is reviewed in the build.
  • Completing a task adds its minutes to the time bank right away. Minute values are the brief's examples (make the bed +2 min, walk 15 min = +10 min, focus block +15 min), hardcoded. The difficult custom task adds extra minutes.
  • Bonus minutes for completing all of the day's tasks.
  • A Time Bank view with the accumulated minutes and the motivating message.
  • Everything saved in the browser's localStorage.
  • Values still to define in the build: the extra minutes for the difficult task and the bonus for completing the day.
  • If 1a slips, the difficult custom task and the day bonus are the first things to move to 1b.

Phase 1b — in scope

CHOOSE and REGISTER: closes the loop, so a full cycle works.

  • Taking time: the user uses all or part of the accumulated time. When time builds up, the app shows messages that ask what they want to do with it.
  • Registering the moment with a note: written, or a short voice note if it proves feasible in the browser. Open: is registering required to keep earning, or optional? To be decided before 1b.

Later phases — not in Phase 1a or 1b

Captured so they are not lost. Each enters only through the change control below.

  • Protected mode while taking time. A web app cannot block other apps, so this needs a native app.
  • Registering the moment with a photo. It needs device access and raises privacy questions.
  • A personal feed of what the user registered, as a reminder of what they enjoy.
  • Editing or deleting predefined tasks, and any other task-management UI.
  • Suggestion system, simple emotional reflection and user-created activities. Described in the brief, not in Phase 1.

Out-of-scope Activities

  • Protected mode, photos, suggestion engine, emotional reflection.
  • Sync, accounts, backend.
  • Editing or deleting predefined tasks.
  • Monetization and any commercial goal in this phase.
  • Game mechanics and retention design: not decided yet, carried to Discovery (step 1.4).

Acceptance Criteria

Phase 1a is done when:

  1. The app has a catalog of 10 predefined tasks across the three categories. Each day it shows up to 3 of them, and lets the user add one custom task per day, flagged as the difficult one.
  2. Marking a task complete adds its minutes to the total right away. The difficult custom task adds extra minutes.
  3. Completing all of the day's tasks adds bonus minutes.
  4. The total is still there after reloading or closing the browser.
  5. The Time Bank view shows the accumulated minutes and the motivating message, with a visual that makes the minutes tangible (idea: minutes filling a clock; to be designed in the build).

Phase 1b is done when:

  1. The user can take all or part of the accumulated time, and the total goes down by the amount taken. When time builds up, the app shows a message asking what they want to do with it.
  2. The user can register the moment with a note, and the note is saved.
  3. A user can complete one full cycle: do a task, earn minutes, accumulate them, take time, register the moment.

The tone of the brief stays as design intent. It is not an acceptance criterion.

Validation Criteria

Phase 1 validates the mechanic and the points model.

  • Points model: the minutes per task, the day bonus and the difficult-task extra are evaluated during Phase 1, starting with 1a, where they already work. The goal is to check they feel fair and motivating. Changes are written into the SOW.
  • Phase 1 signal: the app is used on 5 of 7 days for 2 weeks.
  • MVP success: the user completes at least 3 full cycles in a week.
  • Signals to observe: return next day and within the week, use after 30 days, tasks completed, tasks converted to minutes, minutes used for an activity, moments registered. With a cohort of one they are counted, not turned into percentages.

Change Control

Open Items (carried forward, not resolved at kickoff)

  • Audience, evidence for the problem, market and language → Discovery (1.4).
  • What makes people come back (game mechanics, retention) → Discovery (1.4).
  • Is time earned or free to use? Earning time before using it can feel like "earning the right to rest", which the brief rejects → Discovery (1.4).
  • Privacy and sensitive data (Health tasks, registered moments, photos): moot in Phase 1, because data never leaves the browser. Revisit before any photo, sync, account or backend.
  • Non-functional requirements (accessibility, offline, localization): decided when the build starts.

4 Recovered-time rules missing · Time bank undefined · Recovered vs. real time How does recovered time work? Minutes are the currency. Time accumulates and never expires: there is no way to know what keeps a user from taking it, so it stays in the bank, and the app asks what they want to do with it as it builds up. The user takes all of it or part of it, and the total is always visible. Minute values come from the brief (make the bed +2 min, a 15-minute walk +10 min, a focus block +15 min), hardcoded in Phase 1. Decided
